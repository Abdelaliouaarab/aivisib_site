# How to know whether ChatGPT recommends your brand

To know whether ChatGPT recommends your brand, you have to ask it the questions your customers ask — not questions about you — in a neutral session, several times, and count how often your name comes back. Asking your own ChatGPT once tells you almost nothing: your account's memory, your past conversations and your location shape the answer you get, and a single answer is a coin toss, not a measurement.

This guide gives you the method, step by step, including the version you can run by hand for free.

## What "recommended" actually means

When someone asks an assistant *"best dental clinic in Muscat for families"* or *"which AI visibility tool should I use"*, the model writes a short answer naming two to five businesses. Being recommended means being one of those names.

Three things matter, in this order:

- **Whether you appear at all.** Most brands score zero on the questions that matter to them, and never find out.
- **Where you appear.** First name cited and fourth name cited are not the same commercial reality.
- **Who appears with you.** The list of names next to yours is your real competitive set in the eyes of the model — often not the one you had in mind.

## Why your own ChatGPT gives you a false reading

This is the most common mistake, and it works in both directions: it can invent a success that does not exist, or hide one that does.

- **Memory.** If you have ever mentioned your company to ChatGPT, it may reuse that. You are being shown your own reflection.
- **History.** Past conversations bias what comes next, even without explicit memory.
- **Location and account.** The same question asked from Casablanca, Paris or Dubai does not return the same businesses.
- **A single draw.** Ask the same question three times and you will often get three different lists. One answer measures nothing.

The fix is simple: use a logged-out or temporary chat, and ask several times.

## The method, step by step

You can do this by hand. It costs nothing but time.

1. **Write the questions your customers would type.** Not *"what do you know about AIVisib"* — that question guarantees a mention and proves nothing. Write *"best AI visibility tool for a small agency"*. Cover the intents that matter: best, near me, price, comparison, reputation. Ten to twenty questions is enough to start.
2. **Open a neutral session.** Log out, or use a temporary chat. Turn web search on if your assistant offers it, since that is what most customers now use.
3. **Ask every question at least three times**, in separate sessions. Three is the minimum that lets you tell a real signal from noise.
4. **Record, for every answer:** were you named, in which position, and which competitors were named.
5. **Compute your score:** the number of answers naming you, divided by the total number of answers. If you were named in 7 out of 30 answers, your visibility on that set of questions is 23%.
6. **Repeat in each language you sell in.** A brand can be visible in English and invisible in French or Arabic. This is one of the most frequent blind spots.

## One answer is not a measurement

This is the part most people skip, and it is the one that decides whether your number means anything.

If you ask a question three times and you are named once, your measured score is 33%. But with three draws, the real value could plausibly be anywhere from about 5% to 80%. That range is the margin of error, and it shrinks as you add answers. With 300 answers instead of 30, the same 23% becomes a figure you can defend in front of a client.

So a serious measurement always states two things: the score, **and** its precision. Any tool or report that gives you a bare percentage with no margin of error is hiding how little it knows. ## Do not stop at ChatGPT

ChatGPT is the most used assistant, not the only one your customers open. Gemini sits inside Google. Perplexity is built for search. Claude is widely used at work. The same question often produces genuinely different lists across them — a brand strong on one can be absent from another, usually because the sources each engine leans on are different.

Measuring one engine and calling it "AI visibility" is like measuring one country and calling it your market.

## What actually moves the number

Once you have your score, the work is to change it. What we see move results, in rough order of effect:

- **Being present on the sources the engines read.** Assistants with web search quote pages: comparisons, directories, reviews, specialised articles. If your category's reference pages never mention you, no amount of work on your own site will fix that.
- **Pages that answer the exact question.** A page titled with the buyer's question, answering it plainly in the first paragraph, is far more quotable than a brochure page.
- **Consistency of your facts.** Name, activity, cities, services, prices: if they differ across the web, models hedge, and a hedging model recommends someone else.
- **Covering your languages.** Publishing in a language you sell in, and measuring it, often produces the fastest visible gain.
- **Being readable by machines.** If your site's text is injected by JavaScript, most AI crawlers see an empty page. It is worth checking — we discovered it on our own site, and we published what we found.

Expect four to eight weeks before a change shows in the answers. Models and indexes do not update on the day you publish.

## When a tool is worth it

Doing this by hand once is useful and instructive. Doing it every week, across four engines, three languages and twenty questions, is 720 answers to read and count. That is where a tool pays for itself — not for the magic, for the counting.

Whichever tool you choose, ours included, ask three questions before paying: which engines are actually included in the plan you are buying, how many times each question is asked, and whether the score comes with a margin of error. If any of the three has no clear answer, you are buying a number, not a measurement.
