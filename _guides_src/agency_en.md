# AI visibility for an agency: how to track ten clients without drowning

The short answer: one project per client, a fixed set of twenty to forty buyer questions each, measured weekly rather than daily, and a report that states its own margin of error. What decides whether this becomes a service line is not the tool — it is your cost per client and the hour you spend reading each report.

This guide is the operational version: how to set it up, what to charge, what to put in the client report, and what to refuse.

## What you are actually selling

Not a dashboard. Your client can buy a dashboard. What they cannot buy is someone who reads it and says: *this month you lost the "best X near me" question to a competitor, here is why, here is what we publish next.*

So price the reading, not the access. The measurement is your raw material; the recommendation is the product. Agencies that resell access alone get compared on price and lose. Agencies that deliver an interpretation and a plan keep the client for years.

## The setup, client by client

**One project per client, never one shared project.** You need separate histories, separate competitor sets, and a report you can hand over without anyone else's data in it.

**Twenty to forty questions per client.** Fewer than twenty and the margin of error swallows the signal. More than forty and you pay for precision nobody will read. Cover the intents that matter commercially: best, near me or worldwide, price, a specific service, reputation, and one or two situations typical of the sector.

**Freeze the question list.** This is the rule agencies break most often. Change the questions and you are measuring a different thing; the history becomes meaningless and the client's chart lies. Set the list, agree it with the client, and touch it only at a declared reset.

**Declare the languages the client actually sells in.** A brand can sit at 40% in English and 0% in French or Arabic. That gap is often the easiest win you will ever deliver, and nobody finds it without measuring it.

**Set the scope honestly.** A restaurant has a city. A software company sold worldwide has none, and forcing a city on it distorts every question. Pick one.

## What goes in the client report

Four things, in this order, and nothing else.

**The number, with its precision.** "You are cited in 23% of answers, ±5 points on 300 answers." The margin is not a weakness to hide; it is what makes the number defensible when the client's boss asks.

**Who is cited instead of you.** The competitor list the engines produce is often not the one the client believes in. That single slide changes conversations.

**What moved, and whether it is real.** On a few hundred answers a week, anything under eight points of movement is noise. Say so. A client who learns from you that a five-point gain means nothing will trust you the day you tell them a twelve-point gain is real.

**Three actions, not thirty.** Pages to publish, sources to appear on, a language to cover. Prioritised, with the reason attached to a measured gap.

## The five mistakes that lose clients

**Promising a position.** Nobody controls what a model answers. You control the inputs — pages, sources, consistency, languages — and you measure the effect. Say that in the first meeting, in writing.

**Reporting daily.** A daily number on a normal question set moves by ±9 points on its own. You will spend your month explaining noise, and the client will conclude you are guessing.

**Changing the questions when the score is bad.** It works once. The second time, the client notices the chart restarted, and you have lost the only thing you were selling: credibility.

**Reselling raw access.** The moment the client logs in themselves and sees the same screen you invoice for, your price becomes the tool's price.

**Taking a client who cannot move.** Which brings us to the last point.

## When to say no

Refuse the client whose brand is three weeks old with no website, no reviews and no content. There is nothing to measure and nothing to move; you will show them zero for three months and lose them angry.

Refuse the client who wants weekly guarantees.

Accept the client who has real activity, real customers, and a market where competitors are already being recommended. That client is at 5 or 15%, not 0%, and every point you gain is visible, defensible and worth paying for.

## Two practical details that save hours

**White label from the start.** Your name and logo on the report, not the tool's. It is not vanity: it keeps the conversation on your work.

**Send a read-only link, not a PDF, and not a login.** The client sees the latest version without an account, you see whether they opened it, and you stop chasing attachments. For clients who want something on their own site, a badge showing their score and its date does the job — and it works for you too, since it links back to the report you produce.
